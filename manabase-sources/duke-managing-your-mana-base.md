Mana is one of the fundamental resources of Magic. Without it, you can't play the game. Every aspect of Magic*, *from deckbuilding to sideboarding to gameplay, requires a deep understanding of mana.

Looking at a deck or strategy without the context of its mana base can sometimes lead you seriously astray. Building a mana base is an aspect of deckbuilding that's neglected by players of all formats and levels of experience.

There are no hard and fast rules about what a mana base should look like; any guidelines I can offer might need to be adjusted or completely scrapped under certain circumstances. My hope is to give you some useful starting points, and intuition for when you should deviate from them.

_Header - Math_

In my opinion, some of our very best offerings here on ChannelFireball.com are Frank Karsten's math-based articles on a variety of Magic-related topics. Frank can answer all of your questions regarding the probability of mana working out under different conditions.

There's no need for me to reinvent the wheel. Instead, I'll link you to a few of Frank's most relevant articles, and we'll use them as a baseline for further discussion.

_Header - How Many Lands to Play_

For a normal-looking Standard deck, Frank recommends 25 lands. This would be a 60-card deck that badly wants its first three land drops, and also has some cards that cost four and five mana. He tells us that, on the play, the probability of making your first three land drops would be roughly 90 percent, and the probability of making your first four land drops would be roughly 75 percent.

For a normal-looking Limited deck, he recommends 17 lands to achieve similar probabilities.

Notice that even following these recommendations, there's still a fail rate. There's nothing particularly special about having a 90 percent probability of success. You'll still miss land drops sometimes; you'd still love that number to be a little higher.

The reason we consider these land counts to be best practices is that they strike a healthy balance between drawing enough lands, but not too many. If you never wanted to miss land drops again, you could play 50-something lands in your deck, but then you'd have so few spells to work with that you'd lose for a different reason.

As we discuss mana, we have to do so with the understanding that nothing is perfect. We're making tradeoffs between power and consistency, between the risks of mana screw and mana flood.

When it comes to mana, you shouldn't think in terms of *good enough*. There's no such thing as *good enough. *You should always strive to make your mana more reliable, so long as you can do it without making unacceptable sacrifices elsewhere. And the more things that can go wrong, the less often things will go exactly right.

With that background, let's go over a few things that might cause you to break from these recommended land counts.

### Higher or Lower Mana Curve

If your deck is full of cheap spells and you don't need to hit your fourth and fifth land drop, then you can afford a lower land count. Conversely, if you need to hit all of your land drops even into the late game, you should play more than the baseline number.

### Nonland Sources of Mana

Nonland sources of mana can lead you to reduce your land count, but you shouldn't count them directly as lands. For one thing, you can't cast Wolfwillow Haven unless you already have access to two mana, including green. For another, your Jaspera Sentinel might be killed by an opposing Stomp. Perhaps most importantly, you're playing these cards with the goal of jumping ahead in mana, and missing land drops is counter to that goal.

### Card Drawing & Card Selection

Scrying and drawing extra cards can help you regulate the number of lands that you draw. Note that this cuts both ways. If you haven't drawn enough lands, you can use card drawing and card selection to find more; if you've drawn too many lands, you can use card drawing and card selection to find spells to cast.

This can make it very hard to tell how many lands you should be playing when your deck has lots of Opts, Brainstorms and Faithless Lootings. You might be playing less than the optimal number of lands, and yet you might not notice yourself getting manascrewed because your card selection spells are bailing you out.

Nevertheless, if you have to constantly use your card selection to *find* lands, you might be making things harder on yourself than they need to be. Be careful not to cut too low on lands in decks that fit this description. If you're playing a Delver of Secrets deck that wants to scry every land beyond number two to the bottom of the library, then sure, go as low as 19 or 20 lands. But if you still want to hit four, five or more land drops, then there's no reason to press it this low.

The specifics of your spells matter too. A blank "draw a card" as it appears on Reach Through Mists, Mishra's Bauble or Wall of Blossoms can result in flooding. You may keep a normal-looking opening hand with a few of these cards, only to have them deliver lands instead of spells. If you have a high number of this style of cantrip, you should reduce your land count a little bit.

Spells that are heavier on card selection, like Preordain or Anticipate, don't have this problem; if you have enough lands already, you now gain the luxury of choosing the best spell you see. These can still help you hit your land drops in a pinch, and you can therefore consider a slight trim on total land count. However, you shouldn't dramatically change the structure of your deck just because you're playing Omen of the Sea.

Perhaps more importantly, you need to consider the mana cost of your card drawing spells. Spells that cost zero or one mana can help you keep land-light opening hands, but more expensive spells cannot. Something like Graven Lore should definitely not cause you to lower your land count, because you won't even be casting it until you've found five lands! If anything, Graven Lore should cause you to raise your land count, since it helps you have a plethora of action in the late game and avoid manaflood.

_Header - Mana Screw and Flood_

Every new player learns these concepts early in their Magic career. These things can happen to anyone, with any deck, at any level of competition - they're simply part of the game.

That said, there are techniques you can use in both deckbuilding and gameplay to manage these risks. Doing a good job building your mana base and mana curve is step one, but here are a few other tricks you can use.

### Mana Sinks

Build some ways to use extra mana right into the structure of your deck. Once you do, you can afford to play a slightly higher land count, thereby reducing the risk of mana screw and mana flood simultaneously.

### Cheap Cards

To mitigate the effects of mana screw, play with cheap cards that can answer or trade with more expensive cards from your opponent. Lowering your mana curve is one of the best ways to improve the consistency of your deck, and the quality of your opening hands.

A mana base is not just about your number of lands and mana sources, it's also about the color requirements you can support. Personally, as a way of organizing my thoughts, I always start by asking, "how much do I need each color of mana?"

Your standards should be different for Constructed and Limited play. Constructed formats are faster and more powerful; being unable to cast your spells on time will cause you to lose games. You should still strive for consistency when you play Limited, but some amount of imperfection is going to be built into the format.

_Header - The Scale of Colored Mana Necessity_

### I don't want lands in my deck that can't cast this card.

There's little sense in the question, "How much black mana do you need for Phyrexian Obliterator?" The answer is: "All of it."

You can't cast Emergent Ultimatum on time if you have a land that doesn't produce green, blue or black mana. This is a big reason why Standard Sultai Ultimatum decks don't play with a basic Mountain, despite having access to Fabled Passage and Tibalt, Cosmic Impostor.

### I don't want to keep an opening hand without this color of mana.

This is where a deck is entirely or almost entirely centered around a single color. You cannot operate at all if you don't draw this color of mana.

### This is a main color.

A main color is one that you're counting on seeing in every game. You hope to see it in your opening hand, but you're not automatically compelled to mulligan if you don't.

When you're dealing with multiple colors that you really want to draw every game, you'll maximize your chances of drawing all of your colors by splitting your lands evenly among them. For a R/G Limited deck, you should default to a 9-8 split of Mountains and Forests unless your colors are skewed in a fairly extreme way.

### This is a secondary color.

A secondary color is one that you're planning on seeing, but doesn't necessarily need to be right away. It's within the realm of possibility to win a game without ever drawing this color of mana. You probably don't have double-costs of a secondary color, at least not among your cheap spells.

### This is a splash color.

Splash colors come up often in Limited, and more rarely in Constructed. Still, it can come up with an activated ability, a sideboard card for a slow matchup or a card that you've put in your deck with the intention to search for it, but would like the option to cast it in case you draw it.

_Header - The Cost of Adding Colors_

Adding colors is an example of the tradeoff between power and consistency. The more colors you play, the wider the range of cards you have access to. However, playing more colors also leaves more room for things to go wrong.

### Enters the Battlefield Tapped

One cost of playing multiple colors is having your lands enter the battlefield tapped. A Mountain will always provide mana the turn you play it. Inspiring Vantage, Clifftop Retreat and Temple of Triumph may not.

The true cost of lands entering the battlefield tapped - and how many you can afford to play in your deck - depends on your exact strategy. An aggressive deck that wants to play a one-drop, a two-drop and a three-drop literally cannot do so if one of its lands enters the battlefield tapped. This is why aggressive decks are best served with conservative mana bases in both Constructed and Limited. On the other hand, if you're playing a slower strategy with no one-mana spells at all, you hardly mind playing a tapped land on the first turn.

When it comes to my personal philosophy, I always say that I'd rather cast my spells a turn late than not cast them at all. As such, I'm more likely to cut a color from a deck than to add an extra color. If I do have demanding mana requirements, I'll often play one or two more tapped dual lands than what might be "stock" (this is particularly true if it's a value land like Shambling Vent or a Triome).

### Paying Life

Sometimes you get mana fixing at the expense of paying life. Again, the true cost of this may vary. I'll just say that you shouldn't underestimate the accumulation of life loss, even against opponents who aren't aggressively attacking your life total. It can be a big deal to be deep into a long game and look down to find yourself at thirteen instead of twenty.

### Making a Choice

In a two-color deck with fairly easy mana requirements, you might not notice much difference between Needleverge Pathway and Plateau. However, if your deck has double-costs in both of your colors, or if you're playing three or more colors, then these two lands are most definitely not the same.

When you play Needleverge Pathway, you need to choose between producing white mana or red mana, and you cannot switch from turn to turn. A deck like Standard Naya is interested in having WWGGRR so that it can cast any Adventure spell-plus-creature combination in one turn. With a mana base of all basic lands and Pathways, this is a very difficult thing to achieve. Particularly if your opening hand is light on lands, it can be painful to commit to one color and not the other.

Similarly, if you're considering splashing a black card into your Jeskai deck off of four Fabled Passages and one Swamp, consider what this means in practice. Are you happy to commit to using Fabled Passage for an off-color land? If the answer is yes, then it may negatively impact your ability to produce your main colors. If the answer is no, then you might find yourself firing off Passage for an Island in the early turns, and then being unable to cast your splash card later.

_Header - Examples_

By way of example, I'll highlight the two decks that I registered for the recent Strixhaven Championship. My teammates and I had some interesting and challenging discussions about the mana in each.

### Gruul Magda

Five of us agreed on playing Gruul Magda. What we couldn't agree on was Raph's last-minute idea of splashing Disdainful Stroke as a weapon against Sultai Ultimatum. Should we play it in the main deck? The sideboard? Or not at all? And how would each of these choices mesh with our mana base?

As a starting point, we had access to a number of ways to produce mana of any color. Jaspera Sentinel and Magda, Brazen Outlaw can do it on the cheap, but they also get killed often. Hall of Oracles can do it, but inefficiently. Goldspan Dragon into Disdainful Stroke is extremely powerful, but are we already on track for a win if we're attacking with an uncontested Goldspan Dragon?

Next, Raph made the point that there's no strategic downside to swapping basic lands for blue Pathways. We didn't expect to face Field of Ruin (if we did, we could always play one Mountain), and Standard doesn't have cards like Blood Moon or Price of Progress to punish nonbasic lands.

Here, however, it's crucial to recognize that Barkchannel Pathway is not the same card as Tropical Island. It can produce blue mana, but it doesn't *incidentally *produce blue mana; it forces you to commit to blue mana. Personally, I didn't think it was realistic to make such a commitment in a deck that's otherwise entirely red and green. If permission spells were a big part of the game plan, and I already had Disdainful Stroke in my hand, then sure, I'm happy to play a Tidechannel Pathway. But to play a blue Pathway preemptively, just in case I later drew my one-of Disdainful Stroke seemed farfetched.

In the end, Kai decided to play Disdainful Stroke in the main deck, but he also added Ketria Triome to his mana base. Raph decided to play Disdainful Stroke in the sideboard, and his deck list is referenced above. Gab, Andrew and I decided to stick to plain R/G with no splash.

I think all three choices are reasonable, and create an interesting picture of what you can make a mana base do. Disdainful Stroke is a very powerful card, and I think the splash would definitely be worth it if you expected to face mostly Sultai. But for an open field, I personally decided to spend my sideboard slots elsewhere, and avoid the risk of being stuck with a blue card and improper mana to cast it.

### Jeskai Turns

Our other deck was Jeskai Turns in Historic.

Yet again, there was a splash color, which is somewhat rare in Constructed *Magic. *Instead of splashing a sideboard card, this deck splashed Velomachus Lorehold. In a perfect world, this creature would go directly onto the battlefield via Indomitable Creativity. However, it could also come up that you've naturally drawn it, and that the game has gone long enough to cast it for seven mana.

Raugrin Triome was a slam dunk, since it's a quality U/R dual land that incidentally produces white mana. You can also make Treasures via Magma Opus or Prismari Command. All told, we could cast Velomachus on seven mana more often than not, especially if we planned ahead a little bit.

What about going deeper into white for some nice sideboard cards, or all-stars like Lightning Helix or Teferi, Hero of Dominaria? It was possible, but would've come at a cost. We could've played a basic Plains to pair with Fabled Passage, but how often would we want to make the commitment of searching for that Plains early? How inconvenient would it be to actually draw it? Similar questions applied to Needleverge Pathway or Hallowed Fountain.

Another crucial factor was Dwarven Mine. One of the most powerful draws this deck could deliver was turn four Dwarven Mine untapped, making a token, into Indomitable Creativity targeting that token. Every land that's not a Mountain reduced the chances of playing Dwarven Mine untapped on turn four. So how could we balance a healthy number of blue sources with plenty of Mountains? What about tapped vs. untapped lands?

I quickly identified that I wanted to start every game with an Island/Mountain dual land. If I didn't, then I'd either be missing blue mana, or I'd have to use Fabled Passage for Island and negatively impact my Dwarven Mines. Steam Vents and Raugrin Triome were no-brainers, but I was even happy to complement them with two more Ketria Triomes.

Next, we had to think about post-sideboard games, where you often need multiple blue lands on the battlefield to fight permission battles with Mystical Dispute. Being able to fetch a second Island was important. Moreover, since nearly every land in the deck produced Red, Island was hardly worse than Sulfur Falls or Riverglide Pathway.

Should four Fabled Passages be automatic in a multicolor deck with Brainstorm? Unfortunately, Dwarven Mine and Fabled Passage compete as lands that enter the battlefield tapped prior to turn four, but untapped after that. Maxing out on Mines, Passages, and Triomes makes it hard to cast your two and three-mana defensive spells on time. It also hurts the chances of the deck's other great opening of turn three Mizzix's Mastery on Magma Opus. In other words, we identified important patterns where having too many Fabled Passages would hurt us.

That's how we settled on this odd-looking, Mountain-heavy mana base featuring two Islands and three Fabled Passages, but no Sulfur Falls or Riverglide Pathways. I hope these examples have given you an idea of some of the important questions to ask about your own mana base.
